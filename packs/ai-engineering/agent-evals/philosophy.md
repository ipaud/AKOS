# Philosophy — Agent Evals

## The discipline already exists; only the assertion is new

Nobody merges a change to a payments module on the strength of "I tried it three times and it looked right." The reason is not that payments are important and agents are not — it is that thirty years of practice built an apparatus for the deterministic case and made using it cheaper than skipping it. Agent work has no such apparatus yet, so the anecdote survives, wearing a lab coat: a few prompts, a side-by-side, a confident sentence about the new version being better. The right response is not to invent a new epistemology for language models. It is to notice that almost everything from testing transfers — fixed inputs, a runner, CI, red before green, coverage as a question worth asking — and that exactly one component doesn't: the assertion. A deterministic system lets you write `assertEqual`. A judgment-laden one requires a grader that tolerates variation and still says something falsifiable. Everything hard about evals lives in that one substitution, and everything else is a solved problem being re-solved badly.

## Measurement changes what gets built, whether or not anyone intends it

An eval suite is not a passive instrument. It is the definition of "better" that every subsequent change gets optimized against, by humans as much as by any training loop. Whatever the suite measures will improve; whatever it doesn't will drift, and the drift is invisible because nothing reports it. A team that measures answer quality and not token spend does not decide to build an expensive system — it builds one anyway, one accepted change at a time, each individually justified. This makes the choice of what to measure a design decision with more downstream force than most architecture decisions, and it makes "we'll add cost metrics later" a statement about which system will exist by the time later arrives.

## A number's authority comes from what it was compared against

There is a strong instinct to treat a percentage as self-evidently meaningful — 74% feels like knowledge in a way that "it's noticeably better" does not. But a bare figure carries no information at all: it is a function of the corpus, the grader, the run count, and the version, and changing any of them moves it. What makes an eval result usable is never the number; it is the comparison the number takes part in, held under conditions where only the thing being tested changed. A suite that reports figures without baselines produces confident-looking artifacts that cannot support a decision, which is worse than an honest "we don't know" because it stops the question from being asked.

## The most expensive failure is the one everything looked fine during

Agents fail in a specific and inconvenient way: every step is defensible and the outcome is wrong. The tool selection was reasonable, the arguments were well-formed, the intermediate reasoning reads well, and the user did not get what they asked for. A suite built from step-level checks will show green throughout — which is why the outcome question has to be asked directly, in terms an outside observer can verify, rather than assembled from the parts. The same shape recurs one level up: a suite of individually sensible cases can collectively measure nothing, if none of them was ever observed failing. In both cases the pathology is identical — a chain of locally valid checks producing a globally false conclusion — and the defense is the same: verify the whole at the level that matters, not just the pieces.

## Trust in a suite is the actual asset, and it is destroyed cheaply

The value of an eval suite is not the cases. It is that when the suite goes red, someone stops. That property takes months to build and one of two habits to destroy. The first is flakiness: a handful of cases that fail at random teach everyone to re-run, and re-running is indistinguishable from ignoring. The second is the convenient baseline update: a regression made to disappear by re-recording the number it regressed against. Both are locally reasonable — the flaky case does test something real, the baseline did need updating eventually — and both convert the suite from an instrument into a formality. This is why the rules around baselines in this pack are procedural rather than technical. The problem was never that anyone couldn't tell a real regression from noise; it is that under deadline pressure, the path of least resistance decides, and the job of the process is to make the honest path the easy one.

## An eval score is a claim about a sample, and the sample is always smaller than you'd like

Every suite is a curated corpus standing in for a population nobody can enumerate. This is not a flaw to be fixed by growing the suite; it is the permanent condition, and the honest response is to say what the corpus was every time the number is quoted. The failure mode is not small corpora — small corpora are fine and often all that is affordable. It is small corpora reported as though they were large ones, where a technically true "100% recall" invites a reader to hear "catches everything" when it means "every case we thought to write still passes." Stating the corpus alongside the number costs one clause and removes the entire class of misreading.

## Where this philosophy stops

This pack measures an agent's behavior and output quality across runs. It does not test deterministic software — unit, integration, and end-to-end testing of ordinary code is [packs/testing/testing-pyramid](../../testing/testing-pyramid/README.md) and [packs/testing/qa-checklists](../../testing/qa-checklists/README.md), and the relationship is stated deliberately in AE4 rather than blurred: an eval suite is structurally a test suite with a different assertion layer, not a different discipline. It does not score an AKOS review — the twelve-lens rubric, its severity anchors, and its bands are [core/scoring-model.md](../../../core/scoring-model.md)'s, and this pack composes with them rather than restating them. It does not choose an agent's architecture ([agent-foundations](../agent-foundations/README.md)) or assemble its context ([context-engineering](../context-engineering/README.md)), though it is the mechanism by which a change to either is shown to have helped. And it does not license shipping on a green suite: a suite is a sample, its coverage is always partial, and a passing run is evidence about what was measured — never a guarantee about what wasn't.
