# Philosophy — Continuous Discovery Habits Pack

## Discovery is a weekly habit, not a project phase

Most teams treat "user research" as a discrete phase before a big initiative — interview a handful of users, synthesize findings, then build for months without talking to customers again. Torres's central reframe: teams that touch customer reality *every single week*, even briefly, develop calibrated product judgment that infrequent research never produces. The habit compounds — weekly small-N interviews over a year teach more than one large study ever could, because patterns emerge across many independent looks.

## Opportunities, not just solutions, need structured exploration

Teams jump from a customer problem straight to "let's build X" without exploring the *space* of possible opportunities (needs, pain points, desires uncovered in research) or the *space* of possible solutions for each opportunity. The **opportunity solution tree** exists to make that exploration visible and comparative: one outcome at the root, many opportunities as branches, multiple candidate solutions per opportunity as leaves — so a team can compare options instead of anchoring on the first idea that came up.

## Assumptions, not just ideas, get tested

Before committing to build a specific solution, the riskiest assumptions underneath it (desirability, usability, feasibility, viability — the same four risks as [Inspired](../inspired/mental-models.md)) get identified and tested with the smallest possible experiment. Torres emphasizes mapping assumptions explicitly (an assumption-mapping exercise) rather than testing the whole solution as one lump — because usually only one or two specific assumptions are actually risky, and testing all of them together wastes effort on the ones that were never in doubt.

## Interviewing for stories, not opinions

Customer interviews focused on "what do you think of this idea?" produce unreliable opinions (people are bad predictors of their own future behavior). Interviews focused on eliciting specific past stories ("tell me about the last time you tried to do X") produce reliable data about actual behavior, actual pain, and actual context — the technique (continuous interviewing) depends on asking for stories, not soliciting feedback on concepts.
