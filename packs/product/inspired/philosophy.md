# Philosophy — Inspired Pack

## Empowered teams solve problems; feature teams build outputs

The central distinction: a **feature team** is handed a roadmap of features to build, on schedule, and judged on output (did we ship it). An **empowered team** is handed a problem to solve (a business outcome to move) and given latitude to discover the best solution, judged on outcome (did it work). The difference isn't process theater — it changes what engineers and designers are even allowed to contribute, and it's the single biggest predictor of whether a team produces products customers love versus a checklist of shipped tickets.

## Most ideas don't work — discovery is how you find out cheaply

Cagan's blunt estimate: the majority of product ideas, even from experienced teams, fail to deliver the intended value once actually used by customers, and a further share of the survivors need significant iteration to succeed. Discovery exists to learn this *before* the expensive part (engineering build) rather than after — the entire discipline is about running the cheapest possible experiment that answers the riskiest question first.

## Four risks, all must clear

Every product idea carries risk along four independent axes: **value** (will customers choose it), **usability** (can they figure out how to use it), **feasibility** (can it be built with available time/skills/technology), **business viability** (does it work for the business — legal, financial, marketing, sales, etc.). A team that only validates feasibility (can we build it) and skips value (will they want it) is optimizing the wrong risk.

## The PM's job is customer and business expertise, not backlog administration

A strong product manager holds deep knowledge of customers, data, the business, and the industry — not merely translating stakeholder requests into tickets. Product, design, and engineering collaborate as peers throughout discovery, not in a relay where product "specs" and engineering "implements."
