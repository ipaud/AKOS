# Mental Models — Escaping the Build Trap Pack

## The value exchange system

A healthy product organization is a system that continuously exchanges value: the company delivers value to customers (solving real problems), and customers return value to the company (revenue, engagement, advocacy). The build trap breaks this loop by optimizing the *delivery* half (ship more) while losing sight of whether real value crossed to the customer side at all.

## Good product manager vs. bad product manager (Ben Horowitz's framing, adopted)

A bad PM has a list of features handed down from execs/sales and manages a project to deliver them. A good PM understands the underlying customer and business problem deeply enough to determine what should be built and why — and can say no to features that don't serve the strategy. The job title doesn't determine which one a person is; the org's incentive structure usually does.

## Product Kata (four steps)

1. **Understand the direction** — company vision and strategic intent.
2. **Grasp the current condition** — real data on where the product/metric stands today.
3. **Set the next target condition** — a specific outcome to reach, not a feature to build.
4. **Iterate toward it** — the team runs its own experiments (Build-Measure-Learn) to close the gap, learning what obstacles exist along the way.
This is a repeating loop, not a one-time planning exercise — each target condition reached triggers grasping the new current condition and setting the next target.

## Strategy deployment (avoiding the cascade-to-features trap)

Company vision → product strategy (which problems, for whom, why) → portfolio/team-level strategic intent → team-level product kata. At *every* layer, the artifact passed down is an intent/problem/target condition — never a feature list. If a feature list appears at any layer, the cascade has collapsed into the build trap at that point.

## Outcome-based roadmaps

A roadmap organized by outcomes/themes with rough timeframes (now/next/later) instead of specific features with hard dates communicates the *what we're solving* without over-committing to *how* — leaving room for discovery to change the solution while still giving stakeholders visibility into direction and sequencing.
