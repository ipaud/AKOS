# Philosophy — Laws of UX Pack

## Design arguments deserve mechanisms

"It feels cluttered" loses meetings; "twelve undifferentiated choices at the decision point — Hick's Law predicts measurable hesitation, and our funnel shows it" wins them. This pack exists to replace taste-versus-taste standoffs with mechanism-backed reasoning. The laws are compressed experimental psychology: each names a reproducible property of human perception, memory, motivation, or motor control.

## Laws bound the design space; they don't design

No law says what your product should be. They say what humans will do with whatever you ship: hesitate at big choice sets, miss small far targets, forget middles, remember endings. Treat them as physics for the medium — constraints to design *within*, levers to design *with* — never as a generator of designs.

## Misapplication is the main failure mode

Each law has a folklore version that overreaches:

- Miller's 7±2 cited for menu lengths (that's scanning/Hick territory, not memory).
- Hick's used to strip capability from expert tools (practiced users don't deliberate).
- Peak-End used to justify neglecting the middle (the middle still gates completion).
- Aesthetic-Usability used to argue polish *replaces* usability (it masks, then the mask slips).

The pack's principles include the boundary conditions on purpose. A reviewer citing a law must also check its applicability conditions — a law cited outside its mechanism is decoration.

## Quantified cousins of qualitative practice

These laws are the measured versions of what [Krug](../steve-krug/philosophy.md) and [NN/g](../nielsen-norman-group/philosophy.md) teach as craft: satisficing ↔ Hick's under time pressure; conventions ↔ Jakob's; recognition-over-recall ↔ Miller's; one dominant action ↔ Von Restorff. When a craft rule needs defending, its law is the citation; when a law needs operationalizing, the craft packs have the moves.
