# Philosophy — GOV.UK Content Design

## Content is a service, not a piece of writing

The output of content design is not prose. It is a person leaving with the thing they came for: a decision made, a form filled correctly, a deadline understood, a fear resolved. Judged that way, most content fails not because it is badly written but because it was never aimed at anything. "We need a page about X" is a request for an artefact; "people cannot work out whether they qualify" is a request for content design.

This reframing has a hard consequence: **the quality bar is the user's outcome, not the author's craft**. Elegant prose that leaves someone still unsure is worse than four blunt bullet points that end the uncertainty. There is no partial credit for tone.

## The organisation is not the subject

Institutions describe the world as they are structured — by department, by legal instrument, by internal process, by which team owns which system. Users arrive with a task and no map of any of that. When content is organised around the organisation, the user is silently handed a job that belongs to the organisation: work out who is responsible, then work out which of their documents applies to you.

Content design refuses that transfer. The unit of organisation is the user's task, expressed in the user's vocabulary — even when that vocabulary cuts across three teams, two policies and a system boundary. Internal structure is a fact about the provider, not a feature of the answer.

## Plain language is about cost, not capability

The most persistent objection to plain language is that it patronises expert readers. The objection misreads what is being optimised. Reading comprehension is not a fixed trait you either clear or fail; it is a *cost* paid out of finite attention that everyone has less of when they are busy, distracted, tired, ill, frightened, or reading in a second language. Specialists, given a choice, consistently prefer the version that costs less — because their attention is expensive and the content is not the point of their day.

So plain language is not a downgrade for weak readers. It is the removal of a toll that every reader was paying and only some could afford. The elaborate register that feels rigorous is usually doing something else: signalling seriousness, hedging responsibility, or reproducing the shape of the source document.

## Design for the worst moment, not the average one

People rarely read important content in comfortable conditions. They read it after a bereavement, during a deadline, on a cracked phone, on a bus, with a child interrupting, or with someone else reading it aloud to them because they cannot. Some are helped through it by a relative, a caseworker, or a support line — an assisted-digital reality that never appears in the analytics.

Designing for that reader is not charity toward an edge case; it is designing for the moment your content actually gets used. Content that survives the worst moment is effortless in the best one. The reverse is never true.

## Evidence beats preference

Inside any organisation, content decisions default to seniority: whoever cares most, or ranks highest, wins the wording. Content design substitutes a different authority — what people actually search for, what they actually ask support, what they actually fail to do, what they say out loud in research. These sources are cheap, plentiful, and routinely ignored, because they tend to contradict the internally preferred framing.

The discipline is to treat internal preference as a hypothesis with no standing. "We call it an *entitlement review*" is not a fact about the language; it is a fact about the org.

## Publishing is the middle of the work

Content decays. Policies shift, prices change, systems get replaced, the words people use drift, and the answer that was correct at launch quietly becomes wrong. Nobody is alerted, because content fails silently: it keeps rendering, keeps ranking in search, and keeps confidently misinforming.

So a page is a standing commitment, not a delivery. Every page needs an owner, a review date, and an honest answer to: if this becomes wrong, who finds out? Content that cannot be maintained should not be published, and content that has outlived its need should be **retired** — deliberately, with redirects — rather than left to rot. Deleting content is a content design act, and often the highest-value one.

## Where this philosophy stops

This lens is about meeting an information need. It does not cover interface microcopy systems or voice and tone (see [ux-writing](../ux-writing/philosophy.md)), screen-level usability (see [steve-krug](../../ux/steve-krug/philosophy.md)), or the accessibility conformance floor (see [wcag](../../ux/wcag/philosophy.md)). It is also deliberately unromantic about brand: personality is welcome where it costs the reader nothing, and nowhere else.
