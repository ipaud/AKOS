# Principles — GOV.UK Content Design Pack

Durable rules. Violating one requires an explicit, recorded tradeoff.

## GC1 — Content exists to meet a user need

Every page, section and paragraph must trace to a stated user need: a person in a situation who needs to do or know something for a reason. Content that traces only to an internal motive (a launch, a policy, a stakeholder request) is not content design output — it is broadcast, and it competes with the content that does meet a need.

## GC2 — Start from the user's question, in the user's words

The page answers a question that a real person asks, phrased as they phrase it. Terminology is inherited from users, not imposed on them. Where the organisation's term and the user's term differ, the user's term leads and the official term follows in brackets or a sentence — never the reverse.

## GC3 — Organise by task, not by organisation

Content structure follows what people are trying to do. Departmental boundaries, system boundaries, legal instruments and internal ownership are invisible to the reader and must be invisible in the information architecture. If answering one question requires the reader to visit three pages because three teams own the parts, the structure has failed.

## GC4 — Plain language is a cost reduction, not a simplification

Write so the content can be understood on first read by someone with no context, under pressure. This is not dumbing down: experts prefer plain language too, because the cost of decoding is a cost they also pay and would rather not. Precision is never traded away — plain language means removing decoding cost, not removing accuracy.

## GC5 — Front-load everything

The most important thing goes first: in the page, in the section, in the sentence, and in the first three words of every heading and link. Background, context, exceptions and qualifications follow the answer. If something must be read *before* the answer, justify it explicitly — that is a tax charged to every reader.

## GC6 — Write for scanning, not for reading

Assume the page is scanned. Meaning must survive in titles, headings, list items and the first sentence of each paragraph alone. Information that exists only in the middle of a paragraph is information most readers will not receive.

## GC7 — Format is part of the meaning

Prose is one of several formats and rarely the best. Sequential actions become numbered steps; parallel options become bullets; multi-attribute comparisons become tables; conditional eligibility becomes a question sequence. Choosing prose for structured information is a content defect, not a style preference.

## GC8 — Name the actor

Every statement describing an action says who performs it. Active voice is the default because it forces this; passive voice is permitted only when the actor is genuinely irrelevant or unknown, and never in instructions, deadlines, obligations, or consequences.

## GC9 — One page, one need

A page answers one question completely. When a page accumulates a second audience or a second task, it splits. Completeness is measured against the one need, not against everything known about the subject.

## GC10 — Say what people must do, not everything that is true

Content is not an archive of the subject. Include what changes the reader's decision or action; exclude what is merely accurate. Volume actively harms: every extra sentence lowers the chance the necessary sentence is read.

## GC11 — Ban jargon, metaphor and formal filler

Three separate failures, all removed. Jargon transfers decoding work to the reader. Metaphor asks the reader to solve an analogy before getting the fact, and translates and localises badly. Formal filler ("in order to", "please note that", "it should be noted") adds length with no information. Specialist terms survive only where they are the term the reader must know, and then they are defined at first use.

## GC12 — Evidence outranks internal preference

Wording, structure and existence decisions are settled by search data, support and complaint volumes, analytics, and user research — not by seniority or internal habit. "We have always called it that" is a hypothesis with no standing. Where evidence is absent, the decision is provisional and gets a review trigger.

## GC13 — The title is the page's contract

The title states what the reader gets, in their words, front-loaded, and is honoured by the content. Titles are the primary navigation surface in search and in listings, and a title that overpromises or under-describes causes failure long before anyone reads a word of the body.

## GC14 — Publish nothing you cannot maintain

Every published page has a named owner and a review date. If neither can be assigned, do not publish: unowned content becomes wrong silently and is then indistinguishable from correct content. Maintenance capacity is a publishing constraint, not an afterthought.

## GC15 — Retiring content is content design

Content has a lifecycle: publish, review, revise, retire. Removing content that no longer meets a need — with a redirect to the nearest correct answer — improves the estate. Stale content is a defect of the same class as a broken feature, and unreviewed content is presumed stale.

## GC16 — Publishing is the midpoint, not the finish

Content is iterated against evidence after launch: search terms that arrive and bounce, support tickets that repeat, drop-offs, failed tasks. A page that has never been revised after publication has never been tested against reality.
